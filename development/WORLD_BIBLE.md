# Sensorium — World Bible

## The World at a Glance

Two hundred years after the wars nearly finished humanity, the species exists in a state of managed diminishment. The debris field in orbit seals them in — a permanent reminder of what they did to themselves. The cities that survived the wars have been contracting ever since, their outer rings abandoned and slowly returning to rust. The knowledge lost in the data collapses of the war years never fully recovered. Technology functions at a fraction of what it once did. And now, after two centuries of slow decline, the plague has arrived.

Humanity is dying. The only option anyone can agree on is horrifying.

---

## The Earth

### The Orbital Debris Field
The wars spread into orbit. Satellites, weapons platforms, supply stations, communications arrays — destroyed, damaged, left drifting. Two hundred years later the field is permanent. Attempts to clear it have killed everyone who tried. The debris is too dense, too distributed, too fast-moving. Humanity is sealed in.

No colony ships. No evacuation. No reaching beyond.

This is not discussed much anymore. It was a crisis for the generation that watched the last attempt fail. For the current generation it is simply a condition of existence, like weather. You do not waste grief on what cannot be changed.

### The Cities
The cities that survived are smaller than they were. Populations contracted during the wars and never recovered. The outer districts were abandoned — not in a single dramatic event but gradually, building by building, as maintenance became impossible and people migrated inward toward whatever warmth and function remained.

The surviving city has a center that still works, after a fashion. Power is intermittent in the older districts but reliable in the core. Water runs. Food is produced. Medicine is manufactured, though in limited supply and with limited efficacy. The technology is not primitive — it is *diminished*. The knowledge to do more exists in fragments, inherited by scientists who understand less of it than their predecessors did.

The city has no name in common use. People say *the city*. Occasionally *here*. There are references to other places — other cities, other countries — but these communications have been falling silent for decades. London stopped responding four years ago. The silence from the east is older. No one investigates anymore. It is understood that what has happened here has happened there, and possibly faster.

### The Rain
It rains almost every day. Has for decades, perhaps longer — no one living remembers a time when it didn't. The overcast is permanent. The sky is a low, grey ceiling. Occasionally, rarely, there is a break — a few hours of pale light that makes people stop in the street and look up, uncertain whether to call it beautiful.

The drainage systems run constantly. The streets are always wet. Buildings that were not maintained for waterproofing have developed the smell of long damp — a specific mixture of mold, mineral seep, and whatever was in the walls when they were built. It is the smell of the city. Visitors from the outer districts notice it; those who grew up here do not.

This is not discussed as climate change. It is simply what the climate is.

### The Outer Districts
The abandoned rings of the city are not empty. People live there who cannot or will not live in the functioning core — the very poor, the dislocated, some of the rebels. Buildings stand but most are not maintained. There is no power grid, no water service. Those who survive there do so through salvage and community.

The city does not think about the outer districts. There is no policy. There is barely acknowledgment.

## The Plague

### What It Is
The plague is called *ash lung* by ordinary people. The scientific designation is *Contaminant-Derived Fibrotic Syndrome* — CDFS — which names its cause and says nothing useful about what it does.

It is a consequence of the wars — specifically their final phase. The wars lasted roughly 150 years in total, but the worst chemical and biological weapons were deployed in the last major conflict, approximately 80-100 years before the story's present. The immediate casualties were catastrophic. But some of what was released was slower — persistent agents designed to degrade over decades, whose byproducts bioaccumulate through the food chain, through the water, through the simple act of breathing air in a poisoned place. Over generations, the contamination accumulated in human tissue. Ash lung began appearing as an epidemic condition approximately 30-50 years ago. The Institute was founded in direct response.

The lungs scar. Slowly at first — a chronic cough, shortness of breath on exertion, a film of grey when someone coughs into their hand. Over months, the scarring progresses: lung capacity drops, the coughing produces blood, climbing stairs becomes difficult then impossible. The immune system, already compromised by the same bioaccumulation, fails to manage the scarring and begins to fail in other ways. Infections that would once have been minor become dangerous. The body attacks itself.

The mind remains untouched throughout. This is the specific cruelty of ash lung: clarity of thought while the body dismantles itself around it. People describe knowing exactly what is happening to them. Watching themselves decline with full understanding and no ability to stop it.

### How It Spreads
It is not contagious in the traditional sense. It cannot be caught from another person. It is contracted through sustained exposure to the contaminated environment — the soil, the water, the air in certain districts, the food grown in contaminated ground. Everyone in the city carries some level of contamination. Whether they develop ash lung, and when, depends on factors that are not fully understood: genetics, exposure history, where they live, what they eat.

The outer districts are worse. The core is better, but not safe.

### Timeline
Onset is typically in middle age, though it increasingly appears younger. Once symptoms begin:
- **Early stage**: Chronic cough. Mild breathlessness. Grey sputum. Lasts months to a year or more. Person is functional.
- **Middle stage**: Coughing blood. Significant breathlessness. Weight loss and pallor. Increasing infections. Lasts months.
- **Late stage**: Housebound. Oxygen-dependent if equipment is available. Rapid systemic decline. Weeks to months.
- **Terminal**: Days to weeks once late stage is fully established.

Available treatments slow the middle stage marginally. Nothing stops the progression.

### The Scale
Of the tens of thousands of humans remaining in the city, a significant proportion are in some stage of ash lung. New cases appear faster than people die of other causes. The scientists' projections — which vary, because the data is incomplete and the methodology is contested — agree on one thing: at current rates, the city will be functionally depopulated within two to four generations.

*Two to four generations* is the range everyone argues about. The transplant program's advocates use the lower end. Its critics use the upper. The number underneath both positions is the same: it ends.

## The Transplant Program

### How It Was Developed
The Institute for Continued Human Consciousness — the Institute, in common usage — has been working on mind-transfer technology for forty years. The original work targeted artificial substrates: computer systems, mechanical frames. Those experiments failed catastrophically. The transferred minds degraded within weeks, sometimes days, in ways that were distressing enough to halt the work entirely for a decade.

The turn toward biological substrates came from a junior researcher who noted that the failure mode in artificial transfer was not the transfer itself but the *absence of appropriate sensory input*. A human mind, suddenly deprived of the sensory environment it evolved to interpret, would begin to fracture. What was needed was not a more sophisticated machine but a living nervous system — one already wired for survival, sensation, and response.

Rats were chosen because the scientists noticed them. In the most contaminated areas — the outer districts, the abandoned industrial zones — humans were dying faster than anywhere else. The rats were not. They were thriving. The question *why are the rats healthy?* became a formal research priority.

The answer: CDFS is caused by persistent chemical compounds that bind to a specific surfactant protein variant found in human lung tissue, triggering progressive fibrosis. Rats have a slightly different molecular variant of this protein. The binding mechanism does not work the same way in rat lung tissue. Same contaminated air. Different biological response.

This discovery — that rats were physiologically immune to the specific mechanism that was killing humans — is the same discovery that led to the transplant program. Harlan followed the rats to the solution. Their immunity and their suitability as substrate are a single thread.

Central to the program's development was breeding a suitable rat strain. Wild rats live 1-3 years — inadequate for a transplanted human mind. Over 40 years, the Institute ran a dedicated breeding program: selective breeding over approximately 120-160 rat generations (rats reproduce every 3-4 months), combined with optimal controlled conditions. Each generation, the longest-lived individuals became the breeding stock. The result is program rats that live ~15-20 years. No advanced genetic engineering — the technology for that was lost in the data collapses. Patient selective breeding under existential pressure, the old-fashioned way.

Program rats are bred and maintained by the Institute and assigned to approved candidates. They are not available outside the program.

The first successful transfer was made eleven years ago. The mind transferred. The rat lived. Communication was established within days through the implanted readout device. The program has been operating at increasing scale for the last seven years.

### How the Transfer Works
The procedure requires several hours. The subject's neural patterns are mapped in full — a process that takes days of preparatory scanning. On the day of transfer, the subject is sedated. The mapped consciousness is transferred to the implant array, briefly stored, and then written into the rat's nervous system via a surgically implanted integration lattice at the base of the rat's skull.

The subject's human body does not survive the transfer. The mind leaves; the body ceases. There is no going back.

The rat wakes. The transplanted mind wakes with it, into a sensory environment unlike anything it has ever experienced.

### The Communication Device
Every transplanted rat carries a small device — the implant produces electrical signals the device translates into text, displayed on a paired screen carried or worn by humans interacting with the rat. With practice, the transmission becomes near-real-time. The quality of output correlates with the strength of the signal, which correlates with how well the human mind is maintaining itself within the rat nervous system.

Early after transfer, output is typically full sentences, normal vocabulary, recognizably the person. Over time — weeks to months — observers begin to notice changes. Sentences shorten. Responses to sensory stimuli arrive mid-thought. The device goes dark for seconds, sometimes minutes, as the rat mind pulls focus. When the human mind returns, it returns to where it left off — but not always to full fluency.

The program refers to this as *adjustment*. The transplanted describe it differently, when they describe it at all.

### Who Runs It
The Institute operates under the authority of the Council — the city's governing body, twelve people elected from the functional districts. The Council funds the Institute, sets transplant policy, and administers the Oversight Authority, which is responsible for security and enforcement.

The Institute's director is its public face and primary advocate. He has run the program since its operational phase began. He is respected, even revered, by those who believe the transplant is humanity's only path. He is despised by the resistance.

### Social Debate
The transplant is voluntary. This is the program's official position and its genuine current policy. No one is compelled.

The practical reality is more complicated. Ash lung patients who refuse the transplant receive the same limited care as anyone else, which is to say insufficient care. There is no policy of coercion. There is significant social pressure, documented waiting lists, and a city where the arithmetic of survival is understood by everyone.

The resistance movement — various groups, various methods — objects not only to the social pressure but to the transplant itself. Their positions range from the religious (the body is sacred, the transplant is a violation of something fundamental) to the humanist (what survives in the rat is not the person; it is a copy that believes itself the person) to the conspiratorial (the program is something other than what it appears).

## How Rat-Minds Live

### Integration: The Standard Model
Transplanted rats typically continue living in proximity to their human families and communities, at least initially. Buildings in the functioning core have been partially modified: rat-accessible corridors running alongside human ones, rat-scale spaces opened in rooms, communication terminals installed at rat height in common areas. The modifications are imperfect and vary building to building — some were done by the city, most by individuals making do.

A transplanted person's day involves the same communication device, the same interactions with the same people, in the same building they've lived in — but from four inches off the ground, in a body that smells everything and hears what humans cannot. The adjustment is described, consistently, as far more disorienting than the transfer itself.

### The Drift
The early transplants — those transferred in the first years of the program, seven to ten years ago — are no longer, in most cases, living in human buildings.

They say they prefer the infrastructure. The wall-spaces, the drainage systems, the tunnels beneath the streets that were built for maintenance and now serve a different purpose. They say the human world is overwhelming — the scale of it, the noise, the light. They say they are more comfortable with others like them. These are reasonable things to say and some of them may be true.

The program does not discuss this pattern in public. The Institute notes that long-term transplanted individuals "adapt to their new sensory environment" and "may choose living arrangements that better suit their current capacities."

The MC notices what the Institute does not say: the early transplants are not choosing the infrastructure because it is better. They are drifting toward it the way water finds a drain. The rat mind, given enough time, wins the argument. And the human mind — still present, still legible in brief transmissions — does not seem entirely aware that it is losing.

## The Resistance

### What They Are
There is no single resistance. There are several groups operating independently with different philosophies and methods — from purely political (advocacy, public protest, legal challenges) to sabotage (targeting equipment, destroying transplant records) to the violent fringe (attacks on Institute personnel, bombings of transfer facilities).

What they share: a conviction that the transplant program is wrong. That what survives in the rat is not what it claims to be, or that even if it is, the means of getting there are a violation of something that should not be violated.

### What They Know
Some members of the resistance know more than others. There are former Institute employees in their ranks, disaffected technicians, a scientist or two who left under circumstances the Institute describes as personal. Whether any of them know about the suppressed research is, at the story's opening, unknown to the MC.

## What Is Lost

The wars destroyed more than infrastructure. They destroyed the accumulated knowledge of generations — data stores, libraries, research archives, the living expertise of specialists who died in the chaos. Two hundred years of diminishment means the scientists working today are working with incomplete tools, partial records, and gaps they sometimes don't know are gaps.

This is the condition in which the Institute operates. It is the condition in which the suppressed research was buried. It is the condition in which the MC works.

It is also why the cure research is unproven: the methodological framework to test it thoroughly does not fully exist. What the buried data contains is a trail — a researcher's notes, preliminary results, a direction that was promising enough to warrant investigation. Whether it leads anywhere is unknown. Whether it would have led anywhere if it had been followed is unknown. The scientist made a judgment call with the information available to him, in a world where following the wrong lead could have cost humanity the time it needed.

He may have been right. That is the horror.
