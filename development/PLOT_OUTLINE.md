# Sensorium — Plot Outline

*Scene-by-scene working outline. Chapters will be determined during prose draft. Beat numbers are working labels only.*

---

## Minor Characters (introduced here, developed in prose)

**Soren** — Maren's Oversight Authority partner on the investigation. Pragmatic, loyal, not given to doubt. He does his job the way Maren once did: efficiently, without the grief. He is not antagonistic — he is the person she was three years ago. His function is to mirror what she is no longer able to be.

**Voss** — A junior researcher at the Institute's data management division. Not ideologically committed to the program in either direction. She answers Maren's questions honestly and does not fully understand what she has pointed her toward. Her function is the unlocking — she does not know she is the pivot point.

**Dael** — A resident in Maren and Eli's building. Former machinist. Underwent the transplant approximately four months before the story opens. At the story's opening, he is in the period when he is still recognizably himself — visits Eli in the evenings, the device output still shows his cadence and his particular humor. Over the course of Act One and into Act Two, he drifts. He stops coming upstairs. He stops using the common terminal in the lobby. The last time Maren checks, the terminal shows only fragments: a word, sometimes two. His name is the last full thing he sends.

> **CRITICAL STRUCTURAL NOTE — THE LOVE STORY**
> The grief in this story only works if the love works first. The reader must believe in Maren and Eli as specifically, deeply in love before they are asked to watch that love change shape irreversibly. The investigation and the transplant program are the story's architecture. The love is its foundation. Do not allow the investigation plot or the world-building to crowd out the "before" — the ordinary, alive, specific love that makes everything that follows devastating rather than merely sad. See also: CHARACTERS.md ("Maren and Eli — What Their Love Looks Like") and STORY_BIBLE.md ("The Love Story as Structural Pillar").

## Act One — Establishment

**Goal**: Establish the world and the people; force the irreversible change.

---

### Beat 1 — The City
Open in rain. Night. Maren walking back from work — we do not yet know where she works or why. Just the city: wet concrete, drainage channels running fast, the smell of damp stone and rot at the margins. The buildings she passes are declining — not collapsed but *tending toward* collapse. The lights are on in some windows. Rats move along the base of the walls. She watches them the way someone watches something they have learned not to look directly at.

This beat establishes:
- Atmosphere (Blade Runner register: dark, wet, managed decay)
- Maren's voice: observational, contained, dry-precise
- The rats: present and unremarkable, not yet charged with meaning
- The rain: never explained, never attributed, simply *present*

---

### Beat 2 — Home
Maren arrives home. Eli has made dinner. The apartment is small, functional, warm in the way of a place that has been inhabited carefully for years. The cough is established in a moment, not a speech — he turns away from the stove briefly; she doesn't comment; he doesn't apologize. This is their adjustment. They've had fourteen months to make it.

**This beat must do more than establish — it must make the reader love what will be lost.** The following are required in the prose, not as a checklist but as the texture of the scene:

- He has made something she mentioned wanting — weeks ago, once, briefly, without emphasis. She notices. She says nothing. This is their economy: nothing is performed.
- He reads a line aloud from something on the counter — something he's been marking, a passage from a book a student brought in, something that caught him. Not because it is relevant to her. Because he shares things when they catch him. She is writing her report. She doesn't look up. She hears it. He doesn't ask her what she thinks.
- One moment — small, unremarkable from the outside — where she is briefly, fully present with him. Not managed. Just there. He does not remark on it.
- When she comes in, he has cleared the table. He always does this. She sets her bag in the space.

This beat establishes:
- Eli's voice: direct, specific, warm. He lives in language — how he speaks is who he is.
- The relationship: seven years, understood between them without performance — but the reader must *feel* the seven years, not just be told about them
- Ash lung as present condition, not crisis — the crisis has been absorbed
- Dael's name comes up casually — he had a good day on the device; Eli mentions it
- The reader leaves this beat thinking: *I want them to keep this.*

---

### Beat 3 — The Oversight Authority
Maren at work. The Oversight Authority offices — function over aesthetics, institutional lighting, equipment that works but not elegantly. Soren briefs her on the assignment: the sabotage investigation. The resistance has been escalating. Three transplant facilities hit in the last month. Equipment destroyed, transfer records damaged. Most recently: personnel killed.

This beat establishes:
- Maren's role and competence: she's been at this a long time and she's good at it
- The investigation's scope: multiple groups, different methods, the violent fringe at the margins
- Soren as her working partner: pragmatic foil, comfortable where she is not
- The transplant program as the thing she works to protect — and the first layer of her doubt

---

### Beat 4 — Harlan's Briefing
A Council-level security briefing following the latest attack. Harlan addresses the room — calm, measured, without anger. He explains the transplant program's necessity with the fluency of someone who has made this argument for twenty years. The line appears here in context:

> *"The sensorium is malleable. We are not bound to one set of inputs. The pattern adapts; the sensorium is merely the interface."*

He is answering someone who asked whether the transplanted are really still *themselves*. His answer is reasonable, in a briefing room. Maren writes it in her notes. It costs her nothing, yet. She tells herself this.

---

### Beat 5 — Dael (Early State)
Maren encounters Dael in the building lobby — he uses the communication terminal at rat height near the entrance. He's four months in. The output is still full sentences. Something like his old humor. He asks about Eli. She passes the message on.

This beat functions as the story's early warning system, delivered quietly: Dael is what Eli will become. At this point, it reads as a functional, if strange, new arrangement. In retrospect it will read differently.

**Craft note**: Write Dael as a person first. He has a specific cadence, a specific humor, something that is recognizably him. The foreshadowing only works if the reader doesn't feel it working — if they simply meet a person, and like him, and later remember that they liked him. Do not write him as a device. Write him as someone Maren actually has a small, real exchange with.

---

### Beat 6 — First Investigation Contact
Maren follows a thread from the latest attack — a logistics pattern, a supplier connection, something that suggests coordination between resistance cells that seemed previously unlinked. She interviews a witness who is not quite cooperative, not quite hostile. She learns the phrase "the researcher" used by people in the network's margins — someone who knows something the resistance has been built around. She doesn't yet know who this is.

---

### Beat 7 — The Conversation They Haven't Had
Eli and Maren. He's had a medical assessment; the progression is clearer now. He raises the transplant — not for the first time, but differently this time. He is not emotional. He has been thinking. He says: *the mind is what I am. The transplant preserves the mind. I've read what the early transplants say.*

She cannot say what is actually wrong, which is that she watched someone go still mid-sentence three years ago and has never processed what she saw. Instead she argues: the program, the timing, the uncertainty. He listens carefully. He does not agree. Neither resolves it. They go to sleep in the same bed, not touching, both awake.

**Health suspicion seed (required in this beat)**: Somewhere in this scene — briefly, without announcement — Maren registers something she does not say. A cough she suppresses before it starts, or a breath she notices on the stairs coming home. She has been doing this for eight months. She does not say it to him, which is the same category of not-saying as everything else in this scene. He may notice. He does not ask. The reader should feel, in retrospect, that this moment was always here.

**Craft note**: This is not a scene about two people failing to connect. It is a scene about two people whose connection is strong enough to hold the weight of what neither is saying. The reader should feel that they know each other too well to perform hope for each other. The not-touching is not distance — it is each of them trying not to transfer what they're carrying to the other. The love is present in the restraint.

---

### Beat 8 — Inciting Incident
A major act of sabotage: a large transplant facility in the eastern district. Equipment destroyed in a manner that required planning and inside knowledge. Several people killed — not Institute staff but transfer candidates in the pre-procedure wing. People who were *waiting*. The calculus has changed. The Council is demanding results. The resistance has drawn blood in a way it cannot walk back from, and so has the program.

Maren and Soren are at the scene. She does her job. She is very good at it. She finds three things: a supply chain signature she's seen before; an unusual access pattern; and a small thing — a detail that doesn't fit — that she pockets mentally, unresolved.

---

### Beat 9 — Eli's Decision
He has made it. He tells her in the apartment, quietly. He does not ask her permission; he asks her to hear him.

> *"I know what it costs. I'd rather pay it."*

She tries to answer this. She fails. She finds that she cannot say the specific thing — cannot describe the freeze, cannot explain why "adjustment" is not a good enough word — because the words require her to believe it herself, and she has been refusing to believe it for three years.

She cannot stop it. The appointment is made.

---

### Beat 10 — The Transplant Day
Maren does not attend the ceremony. She has not attended one in three years — schedule, she tells herself. She works. Late afternoon, her device signals: the procedure is complete. The body has been cremated. The rat has woken.

She reads the message twice. She puts the device in her pocket. She finishes the report she was writing. Outside the window, it is raining.

**Craft note**: In the prose, two or three sentences — brief, not dramatized — should carry this: she was not at the ceremony, and she knows he would have expected her not to be. Not because he doesn't want her there. Because seven years have taught him the shape of what she can and cannot do, and he did not ask her to be different. That he already knew this about her — that he made room for exactly who she is, even at this — is its own form of love. And its own form of grief.

*End of Act One.*

---

## Act Two — Rising Action

**Goal**: The relationship deteriorates in fragments while the investigation deepens; both threads converge at the suppressed research.

---

### Beat 11 — First Contact with Eli-Rat
The post-transfer recovery room: a small space designed for the transition period, rat-accessible, with a communication terminal at floor level. Maren goes the next day.

She waits. The screen activates. His first message is a phrase she knows — a specific word, something exact that is absolutely his — and she recognizes him immediately. Relief, and horror inside the relief.

He describes the sensory environment: the scale of the room (enormous), the smell of her (complex, specific, different from anything she expected), the whiskers as spatial mapping. He is trying to explain something that has no human frame of reference, and he is doing it with his characteristic precision. He is still Eli. She can hear it.

This beat establishes the communication device as the medium of their relationship now, and the specific devastation of hearing him through it — fully present, fully *him*, in a body she cannot reach.

---

### Beat 12 — Investigation: The Network's Shape
Maren pulls on the supply chain thread from the inciting incident. She follows it into a procurement network, finding a series of intermediaries — people who don't appear in any resistance database but whose actions form a pattern. She starts mapping the network's structure: distributed, careful, philosophically motivated rather than opportunistic. This is not vandalism. There is someone thinking at the center of it.

She presses the "researcher" reference again — hearing the phrase from a new source, this time with slightly more context: *she left the Institute. Twelve years ago. Harlan reassigned her.*

---

### Beat 13 — Second Visit: Whiskers
Eli describes the whiskers in detail — the air displacement, the way vibration maps the room before he turns his head. He says: *"the world is bigger than I thought and smaller at the same time. I can hear you from two rooms away. I can hear your heartbeat, I think. It's fast. You don't need to worry."*

He is still Eli. He still notices what she needs and says it. The tenderness is undimmed. The gap between them is also widening, because what he is describing is something she cannot follow him into.

---

### Beat 14 — Dael (Middle State)
Maren checks the lobby terminal. Dael's output: shorter than last time. Not dramatically — a few sentences instead of paragraphs. He asks after Eli. She passes it on.

**Craft note**: Again, write Dael as a person — someone she knows, however slightly, and the shortening is felt because the person was established. His humor is still there, but it takes longer to arrive. The foreshadowing works through specificity, not announcement.

---

### Beat 15 — The Early Transplant
Investigating the eastern district, Maren encounters one of the program's early transplants — seven years in. He is not in the building where his family still lives; he is accessed through a terminal in a corridor opened into the wall space. The output is very short. Full words, but few of them. She asks how he is. A pause, then: *well.* Another pause: *different.* Then nothing.

This is not dramatized as horror. It is presented as a fact, which is worse. She files a note in her investigation log about his proximity to the resistance's outer district infrastructure. She does not think about what this means for Eli yet, not directly. But she notices.

---

### Beat 16 — Third Visit: The Pauses Begin
Eli and Maren. He's settling in — he seems less disoriented than the early visits. But three times in their conversation he loses a sentence mid-way. He picks it up slightly wrong — the word he'd been building toward replaced by a simpler one. He apologizes twice. The second apology is shorter than the first.

She says: *it's okay.* This is the first lie she's told him in seven years.

---

### Beat 17 — Maren Reaches the Network's Edge
The investigation leads her to a meeting point — a contact in the outer districts who has sympathies but is not fully embedded. He confirms what she's already suspected: the resistance has a philosophical core, not just a violent fringe. He gives her a location. He says: *she'll decide if she wants to talk to you.*

**Structural note**: By this beat, Maren has enough to close the case — enough to hand to Soren, enough to identify the network's leadership to the Oversight Authority. She chooses not to. She goes alone, tells no one, does not log the location. She has made the decision before she opens the door. This is important: when she meets Nadia, she is not there to gather evidence. She has already decided something, though she may not have named it yet to herself.

---

### Beat 18 — THE FREEZE
*[The story's most important scene.]*

Mid-conversation with Eli. He is in the middle of a sentence — something precise, something about the way language feels different now, the spacing of thought in a rat's temporal rhythm, something she has been leaning into — when he goes still.

Not absent. *Oriented.* His body shifts — a fraction, then more, toward the far corner of the room. Not agitated. Focused. Completely, entirely focused on something the room does not appear to contain for her.

The screen goes dark.

She says his name.

Nothing.

She waits. She is very still. She waits for longer than feels reasonable. She says his name again.

Footsteps. Small, fast. He has left the room. The terminal shows nothing. Wherever he has gone — through the rat-access corridor, into the wall-space, following whatever it was — he has taken it with him.

She sits in the empty room for a long time.

The screen activates, eventually: *sorry. came back. something — I don't have the word. a pressure. it needed following.*

She writes: *subject demonstrated expected sensory response.* She is the only one who will read this report. She goes home. She doesn't sleep.

---

### Beat 19 — Maren Meets Nadia
The outer district meeting: an occupied building, a room with no addressable windows. Nadia, in person for the first time: precise, unhurried, not at all what Maren expected from a resistance leader. She makes her argument directly:

*Is that Eli in the rat, or a pattern that believes it is Eli?*

She does not ask it cruelly. She asks it the way a doctor asks a question the patient already knows the answer to.

Maren does not answer. She has been living with the question since the freeze.

Nadia explains her argument (substrate non-independence; consciousness as produced by specific biological configuration; the copy argument). Maren disagrees formally — she is a program defender, professionally and by prior conviction — but she cannot dismiss everything Nadia says. The session ends without resolution, but Maren knows she will need to come back.

**Non-arrest note**: She leaves the meeting knowing she will not turn Nadia in. She knows this is a professional act with consequences if discovered. She knows people died in attacks that Nadia's network enabled. She does not explain herself to the reader — but she must be seen *knowing* what she is choosing not to do. One sentence of this awareness, in her interiority, is enough. The choice must register as a choice, not as a gap in the plotting.

---

### Beat 20 — Fourth Visit: Short Sentences
The careful words are mostly gone. Eli is still present — still *himself* in some irreducible way — but the sentences are short, and what he says has become simpler than who he is. He asks what she's working on. She tells him: the resistance investigation. He asks: *does it help to say it out loud?* She says yes. He says: *then say it.* She does. He listens. When he responds, it takes longer, and what comes out is direct and true and has lost the specific texture of his speech.

She reads each word very carefully now, the way you read something you know is getting harder to read.

---

### Beat 21 — The Archive
Following up on "the researcher" and Harlan's restricted archive — Voss, a junior data management researcher, answers Maren's inquiry about twelve-year-old CDFS research files without fully understanding what the question implies. She identifies a sealed collection under Harlan's administrative hold. She gives Maren the archive location.

Maren accesses it.

What she finds: Nadia's research. Preliminary results, methodology notes, the direction of inquiry. A molecular inhibitor pathway for CDFS fibrosis — not a finished cure, but a promising trail that warranted investigation. The methodology that existed to test it would take years to develop properly. The data is good. The data is real. At the bottom of the file record: Harlan's administrative note. *REVIEWED — REASSIGNED — ARCHIVE.* Twelve years ago.

She sits with this for a long time.

---

### Beat 22 — Act Two Climax: Nadia Sees Her Own Research
Maren returns to Nadia. She has the archive data.

She shows it across the table. Nadia is still before she opens the files. She describes the content of the research before reading it — the specific inhibitor pathway, the protein binding target, the early-stage patient population she was testing. She designed the study. She knows what she was looking for. She knows what she found.

Then she reads it. Everything she remembers is there.

They look at the same data from twelve years apart. Nadia sees confirmation. Maren sees something more ambiguous — she cannot tell from the data whether it would have worked. Nadia cannot be certain either. But it was *buried*. Deliberately. They know this now.

Neither says anything for a while.

*End of Act Two.*

---

## Act Three — Resolution

**Goal**: Force the reckoning. Drive toward the impossible choice.

---

### Beat 22.5 — Soren
Between the archive discovery and the Harlan confrontation, Soren catches up to her.

He has noticed. He is competent — he has been running the investigation alongside her for months, and she has diverged in ways a careful partner registers. He does not accuse. He asks a question she cannot fully answer. Something operational — a gap in her log, a lead she didn't share, a location he knows she visited without noting it. She gives him just enough. A partial truth, a redirect, something that lets him choose to let it go.

He lets it go.

Neither of them says what they both understand.

This scene should be short. Its only job is to make visible the distance she has traveled from who she was — and Soren is the measure of it. He is the person she was three years ago: someone who does the job efficiently, without the grief. She looks at him and sees the version of herself that did not go to that early transfer and come home unable to sleep. She has left that version behind. This is the scene where it is confirmed, quietly, between two professionals who will never discuss it.

The mirror dynamic pays off here: he is not her antagonist. He is her past. And she has walked away from it.

---

### Beat 23 — Harlan
Maren goes to Harlan with the archive data. Alone. She has not told Soren. She has not decided what she is going to do with what she knows — she needs to hear him first.

He does not deny it. He explains.

The calculation: the research would have required at minimum five years to test properly. During those five years, voluntary participation in the transplant program would have collapsed — families would hold off, patients in early stages would wait. The plague would continue. People would die waiting for something that might not materialize. And if it failed — which preliminary research usually did — the program would have lost years of momentum and public trust it could not recover. He had done the math. He chose the certain path.

And then the other argument, the one he has rehearsed less but believes more: the transplant is not a failure mode. It is the next step. Even if the research had worked, even if a treatment had been developed that arrested CDFS, the species would still be living in a poisoned world in diminished bodies with inadequate technology, running out of time in ways that a lung treatment would not address. The transplant is not a survival measure. It is a direction. He did not suppress hope. He chose the better hope.

Some of what he says is not wrong. Maren cannot dismiss everything he tells her. The window of uncertainty in the cure research is real. The cost of false hope is real.

He ends: *"I did not make this decision carelessly. I made it carefully. That is different from making it wrongly."*

She leaves without telling him what she will do.

---

### Beat 24 — Dael (Final State)
Maren checks the lobby terminal one last time. The screen shows a single word. Then nothing. Dael's family hasn't heard from him in two weeks. They think he is in the infrastructure — the eastern drainage system, where a community of early transplants has settled. They say: *he seems okay. He just... prefers it there now.*

She knows what "prefers" means. She has known for a long time.

---

### Beat 25 — Eli's Plea
Maren visits Eli. She doesn't tell him about Harlan or the archive — not directly. But she tells him she has found something, something that changes the shape of the choice she's going to have to make. He listens.

Then he says:

> *"I don't want you to become this."*

Five words. Said carefully, with what he has left. She understands: he does not mean the body. He means the drift. He means the freeze. He means whatever it was that took him from the room mid-sentence and has not fully returned.

He is still there enough to see her clearly. He is using what remains to say the most important thing.

She does not answer. She puts her hand near the terminal. He orients toward the warmth of it.

---

### Beat 26 — The Choice (Final Chapter)
Rain. Night. Maren alone in the apartment.

The archive data sits in her hands — or on her device, its weight the same either way. What she could do: release the data to the public. Force a formal inquiry into whether Nadia's research might have led somewhere. Push for investigation of the inhibitor pathway, even now, even with incomplete methods and diminished technology. This is what Nadia wants. This is what twelve years of resistance was built toward.

What this would mean for Eli: he is already transplanted. The cure, if it existed, only works pre-transplant. This changes nothing for him. He stays in the rat. He continues to drift. Whatever she does with this data, she cannot bring him back.

What it would mean for her: if the research trail is valid, if a treatment can eventually be developed, she would not have to do what Eli did.

She is aware of the irony. The choice about what to do with the data is also, structurally, a choice about her own body.

She thinks about Harlan. She cannot dismiss him entirely.

She thinks about the freeze. She cannot dismiss that either.

She thinks about: *I don't want you to become this.*

She thinks about Nadia's question — *is that Eli in the rat, or a pattern that believes it is Eli?* — and she tries, here, to answer it. She has been avoiding it since the first time Nadia asked it. Now she tries. She thinks about the screen activating on the first morning, and the phrase she recognized immediately, and how completely she heard him in it. She thinks about the freeze: the way his body oriented, the footsteps, the gap in the baseboard. She thinks about *I don't have the word* — him, trying to reach her from something she cannot follow him into. She tries to answer Nadia's question. She cannot. The question is not answerable from where she is standing. It may not be answerable from anywhere.

**Health note (required)**: She is also — somewhere in this scene, without announcement — aware of her own body. The cough she has been ignoring. The suspicion she has not tested. The cure research in her hands is data about a pathway that might have saved Eli, and might, if it leads somewhere, save others, and might — if she is what she suspects she might be — change the shape of what she has to decide about herself. She does not name this directly. It is simply present, the way it has been present for eight months.

She is sitting by the window. It is raining, the way it always rains here. Outside, in the street, a rat crosses a drainage channel without breaking stride — small, purposeful, moving through the dark with a certainty that requires no visible source.

She is leaning toward not going through with it.

She is still there when the light changes.

*End of Act Three.*

## Epilogue

No names. No interior. No confirmation.

Two rats in a drainage channel at night — a real one, a maintenance channel running beneath the eastern district. The prose is pure sensation and behavior: the smell of the water (complex, mineral, carrying information in layers a human nose cannot decode), the texture of the concrete (every irregularity registered through vibration before being registered through sight), the sound map of the underground (distant machinery, water movement two levels below, something in the walls that might be electrical).

They move together without apparent coordination — not mirroring, but in rhythm. One pauses at a junction. The other waits without being asked to. They continue.

The rain, above: audible as a shift in pressure, as a change in the smell of air reaching down from the street grates. The one pauses again. Orients upward, toward the grate, toward the sound and smell of rain. Something in the posture — held, attentive, longer than the instinct would strictly require.

Then they continue, together, into the dark.

*The reader decides.*

## Structural Notes

### Approximate chapter map
- Act One: ~5-7 chapters (Beats 1-10)
- Act Two: ~8-10 chapters (Beats 11-22)
- Act Three: ~4-5 chapters (Beats 23-26)
- Epilogue: 1 standalone section (short — under 1,000 words)

### The word "sensorium" — track its appearances
1. **First appearance** (Beat 4 — Harlan's briefing): clinical, dismissive, reasonable-sounding. The MC records it without reaction.
2. **Second appearance** (somewhere in Act Two — likely Beat 17 or 20): she hears it again. The word costs something now. The person she loves is losing to his sensorium.
3. **Third appearance** (Beat 26 or nearby — Act Three): she thinks the word herself. It has changed meaning. Mark this.
4. **Optional fourth**: in the epilogue, do NOT use the word. Let it remain human, a word that belongs to the story's grief.

### The body horror — how it earns its place
Body horror (vibrissae spatial mapping, scent overwhelm, coprophagy, grooming compulsion, pheromone response) should appear in Eli's descriptions from the device — as *his* attempt to translate his experience to her, not as authorial spectacle. The horror is that he is *precise* about it. He finds the right words. The words are accurate. That is the horror.

### The investigation resolution
The investigation's formal conclusion (who organized the sabotage) is NOT the story's climax. It is resolved mid-Act Three or at the end of Act Two — Nadia's network is identified; Maren has enough to close the case. She chooses what to do with that knowledge. She does not turn Nadia in. This is not dramatized at length — it is a decision she makes and does not explain.

### Pacing notes
- Act One should feel like a world working (badly, under strain, but working). The horror is latent.
- Act Two should feel like something unraveling that has been unraveling all along — each beat a notch further.
- Act Three is short and does not relieve anything. No release. The suspended choice is not a failure to end; it IS the ending.
- The epilogue is the exhale, but the lungs don't fully clear.
