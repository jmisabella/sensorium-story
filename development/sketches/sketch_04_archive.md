# Sketch 04 — Discovery of the Archive
*Beat 21 | Act Two*
*Working draft — not final prose*

---

The Institute's data management wing was in the lower east corridor, two floors below the clinical levels. The corridor lights were on the conservation cycle at this hour — half of them, alternating, which gave the passage a striated quality she had learned to ignore. She had been in this building after hours many times. She knew which doors were alarmed and which were not.

Voss had been apologetic, which she didn't need, and efficient, which she did. A sealed collection under Harlan's administrative hold. Twelve-year-old CDFS research files. Preliminary — that was the word Voss had used — *preliminary*, which meant unfinished, which meant Voss hadn't thought further about it. She had given Maren the archive location and gone back to her own work without asking what the question was for.

Maren found the terminal at the end of the row. She sat down.

---

The file record opened normally. Standard header: date of creation, originating department, principal researcher. The name: N. Vasquez, bioscience division. She noted it. She didn't know the name yet, not in this context. She copied it to her log.

The files were organized in the way of preliminary research: methodology notes first, then early results, then what the researcher had been planning for the next stage. She read methodically, the way she read everything. She was not looking for anything in particular yet. She was reading to understand what she was looking at.

The methodology was careful. The principal researcher had identified a molecular inhibitor pathway — not an approach to the disease's symptoms but an intervention at the progression mechanism itself. The methodology described a specific binding target in the CDFS protein cascade. The early results were limited by sample size, as they always were with preliminary work, but the direction was clear. The data pointed somewhere. The data said: *this is worth following.*

She read slowly.

The data was good.

---

Not good as in certain. Good as in: the methodology was clean, the early results were real, and the direction pointed somewhere. Someone who knew how to read this would have read it and thought: *this needs more time and more work.* Someone who knew how to read this would not have read it and thought: *set this aside.*

She went back to the methodology notes and read them a second time.

The pathway would have required years to develop into anything usable — clinical testing infrastructure that did not exist in intact form, sample populations, controlled conditions, time that the plague was not inclined to wait for. The researcher had seen this; she had written a timeline, a resource estimate, a list of what would need to be in place before it could move forward. The timeline was ambitious. The resource list was long. This was not a cure. It was the beginning of a path toward investigating whether a cure was possible.

Maren held this for a moment.

Then she scrolled to the bottom of the file record.

---

Harlan's administrative note was a single line. Date-stamped twelve years ago.

*REVIEWED — REASSIGNED — ARCHIVE.*

She read it once.

She read it again.

She looked at the date. She looked at the quality of the data above it — the care in the methodology, the early results pointing somewhere real, the researcher's mapped-out timeline, a plan for something that might have become something.

*REVIEWED — REASSIGNED — ARCHIVE.*

She was an investigator. She knew what finding the thing felt like. It had a specific texture — the moment when the pieces assembled into a shape that didn't have another explanation. She had felt it many times. She had learned to hold it carefully, without hurrying, because hurrying was how you got the shape wrong.

She sat with this one for a long time.

---

She thought about Eli. Not the freeze — not that, not yet. The early visits, before the pauses started, when he was still describing everything with his particular precision. He had been trying to give her language for a world she couldn't follow him into, and he'd done it the way he did everything: carefully, with effort, without asking her to notice the effort.

She thought about the freeze. He had been mid-sentence. He had been talking about how language felt different now — the spacing of thought in a rat's temporal rhythm — and then he had gone still and oriented toward something she couldn't perceive, and he had left the room without completing the word he had been reaching for, and she had sat in that room for thirty-four minutes waiting.

He had come back. He had said: *I don't have the word.*

She thought about the note at the bottom of the file.

She did not know if the research would have led anywhere. She did not know if the inhibitor pathway would have worked, if the methodology would have proven viable in a city with this infrastructure and this population and this timeline. She was not a bioscientist. She could not read the data and know with certainty what it was worth. She had read it carefully and it looked like something worth following, but she knew looking like something and being something were not the same.

She knew it had been buried.

She knew what deliberate looked like. This was deliberate.

---

She scrolled back to the header. The name: N. Vasquez, bioscience division.

She had been pulling on the thread of "the researcher" for three months. The phrase had come up first as a reference, then as a context: *she left the Institute, twelve years ago. Harlan reassigned her.* She had followed it through the resistance's margins, through the way the network's philosophical core seemed to be organized around something earlier than the violence, something specific that had happened before the first facility was hit.

She looked at the name.

The data and the person were the same thing.

The researcher who had left and built twelve years of resistance around the conviction that Harlan had buried something. She had no evidence — she had her memory of her own work, which was unverifiable, which was exactly the kind of thing that was easy to dismiss. The work was here. It was real. The note at the bottom was the note she had suspected existed. Maren had the data. She was sitting in front of it. She understood now what it was that Nadia had been carrying for twelve years and had not been able to prove.

She did not know yet what she was going to do.

---

She closed the file record. She left the archive as she had found it — no changes, nothing to indicate she had been inside it. She logged her session time as a standard records inquiry.

She sat for a moment at the terminal in the empty data room, the strip lights half-on in the corridor outside. The Institute's lower levels were quiet around her in the specific way of institutional buildings after hours — not silent, but uninhabited. The distant sound of the city above. The faint vibration of the drainage system in the walls.

The cure, if it had become a cure, only worked pre-transplant. This changed nothing for Eli. The research had been filed twelve years ago and Eli's appointment had been made six months ago and there was no version of these two facts in which one undid the other.

She did not think about herself. She made herself not.

She picked up her bag.

Outside, the rain. She came out through the Institute's service entrance and stood on the step for a moment. She was not ready to go home yet, and she did not have anywhere else to go, so she walked. The drainage channel in the eastern street was running fast and dark. She crossed it. She kept walking. She had walked home in the rain enough times that the rain did not require her attention. She let it not require her attention.

The methodical part of her mind was already moving — the investigator's instinct, the habit of finding the next step. She knew who N. Vasquez was. She knew where to find her.

She had the data. Nadia had twelve years of context. Neither of them could act alone.

She walked until she was ready to go home. Then she went.

She did not sleep.
