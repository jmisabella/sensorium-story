# Sketch 02 — First Contact
*Beat 11 | Act Two, day 1 post-transfer*
*Working draft — not final prose*

---

She had not slept. She left before the building's lights came up.

The Institute's recovery section was not built for visitors. She understood this the way she understood a lot of things about the transplant program — by inference, by the gaps in what she'd been told. The admission nurse gave her a room number with the particular efficiency of someone who had done this many times and had made a professional decision about what to do with her face while doing it. Maren recognized it. She used it herself.

The room was small and clean and functional, in the specific way of spaces designed for a single purpose. A cot against one wall for early post-procedure monitoring — unoccupied; the staff preferred rats to move freely as soon as viable, and he'd been transferred the previous afternoon. A wall terminal at standing height, unused. And at floor level, against the far baseboard: the device. A screen in a low bracket, the calibration notice still visible in the corner, the surface unscratched. Fresh.

She sat on the floor in front of it. She had not thought to bring a chair.

The rain came through the window at an angle. She watched the room. The room was the kind of clean that required effort to maintain and nobody would remark on it.

She waited.

The screen activated.

*you're here. I could hear you in the corridor. I'd been wondering whether I'd know.*

She said his name.

*yes. hold on. I am trying to find the words in the right order.*

That was him. The specific construction — *in the right order* — the teacher's sense that language had a proper sequence and you owed it to the words to find it. She recognized it before she'd processed it. Something moved in her chest. She did not name it.

*the room is very large.*

She looked at the room. The room was not large.

*for reference. your shoes occupy approximately one quarter of my visual field from here.*

She looked at her shoes.

*I want to say first that I am not in distress. the experience is extraordinary. I understand that word is insufficient. I am working on a better one.*

She said: tell me.

A pause — the ordinary kind, the kind she had been listening to for seven years while he assembled what he meant.

*you have a smell.*

She waited.

*this is not a simple thing. I want you to understand it is not simple. you smell of the building — our building, specifically. the mineral quality of the water in the pipes. the particular composition of dust in the stairwells. and rain. you carry a great deal of rain. and beneath that: several things I do not have names for, because I have never needed them. you have always had these names. I simply did not have the equipment to read them.*

She was quiet.

*I knew you were in the corridor before you knocked. I knew before the door opened that you were the one opening it. I wanted you to know that.*

She said: are you frightened?

The pause was longer this time.

*I don't know how to answer precisely. I have been awake since before dawn — the light in here changed by degrees and I tracked it. I have been trying to understand the room.*

He had been in here since before dawn. She had left her building when the hall lights came up, thinking she was coming early, and he had been awake before her, mapping the room, waiting.

*the whiskers are the strangest part. may I tell you about the whiskers?*

She said: yes.

*each one reads the air. the displacement of air from your breathing. the heat signature of your body. the precise acoustic shape of you sitting on the floor — you are a very specific geometry, I can feel the edges of you before I turn toward you. it is as if your sense of your own body extended outward into the room. as if you could feel the space the way you feel your hands. this is not accurate, but it is the nearest I have.*

She looked at him at the far end of the room. He was motionless but not still in the way she meant when she thought the word still — there was an attention in him, a continuous reading of the room, that made his stillness into something active.

She had reached the edge of what she could follow. The smell of the pipes in the north stairwell. The geometry of her body as heat and air displacement. She was listening to someone she knew completely describe a world she could not enter.

*I can hear your heartbeat.*

She said nothing.

*it is faster than usual. I know what your heartbeat sounds like at rest. seven years. I know what it sounds like right now.*

He paused.

*you do not need to explain.*

---

She left when the nurse came to indicate the session should end. She did not know how long she had been there. She had not checked. At some point she had stopped noting the rain and then noticed she had stopped.

She walked back through the Institute's main corridor, past the admission desk, out into the morning. The rain was steady. She stood on the steps for a moment before moving.

He had been the one with all the words today. She had asked him two questions and said his name. He had described her smell, her shape in the air, the sound of her heart. He had told her things about herself she had never known — had never been able to know, had never had the equipment. She had sat on the floor in front of a screen and listened to him be entirely himself, precisely and completely.

She did not know what to do with this.

She had been to the early morning cremation, in the end — alone, twenty minutes, a small room in the Institute's lower floor. It was brief. She had filled out the form they gave her.

He was entirely Eli. She had heard him in every line.

She went home through the rain, and in the afternoon she went back to work, and in the evening she sat by the window for a long time watching the drainage channel run fast in the street below. She did not think about anything in particular. She thought about the way he'd said: *I knew you were the one opening it.* The exactness of it. He had been tracking her through a wall.

She made tea. She let it go cold.

She did not sleep.
