# Sketch 05 — The Confrontation with Harlan
*Beat 23 | Act Two*
*Working draft — not final prose*

---

She went to the Institute in the morning. She had not told Soren.

Harlan's office was on the upper administrative level, north-facing, with a view of the drainage channel and the lower district beyond it. It was a working office — papers in order, not stacked; equipment that was used; a chair positioned at a specific angle to the terminal that indicated someone had been sitting there long enough to know the right angle. She had been in rooms like this before. She knew how to read them.

He looked up when she came in. Something settled in his expression — not surprise, exactly. More like the completion of a prediction.

---

She set the drive on the desk between them.

"You've read it," he said. Not a question.

"Yes."

He looked at the drive for a moment. He did not reach for it.

"The note at the bottom," she said.

"I know which note."

She waited.

He sat back in his chair — not relaxing, not performing ease. Simply settling into the shape of what was coming. He had the quality she had noticed in the briefing room: a man who had been right for a long time and knew what patience was for.

"Ask me what you came to ask," he said.

---

She had prepared this. She had been preparing it for three days, since she'd sat in the archive room with the strip lights and the distant rain. She had organized the questions the way she organized a case: in sequence, closing each avenue before opening the next, leaving no room. She was good at this. It was what she did.

She said: "The research was viable. You saw that."

"The early results were promising," he said. "Preliminary research is regularly promising. That's not what preliminary research is valued for."

"The methodology was sound."

"The methodology was sound. Yes." He said it without qualification. "The development pathway was not. The testing requirements, the infrastructure, the time — you've read the researcher's own estimates. She was not being pessimistic. She was being accurate."

"Five years."

"Five to eight. In conditions that did not exist and would need to be built from components that were not available in this city. You have looked at the Institute's records for this period. You know what infrastructure we were operating with."

She had looked. She knew.

---

"During those five to eight years," he continued, "voluntary participation in the transplant program would have been substantially reduced. Families would have held their people out. Individuals in early progression would have waited — watching the research, weighing it, hoping. Ash lung is not patient. The disease does not accommodate a research timeline." He looked at her directly. "I calculated the likely mortality. People who could have been transferred, who would have been transferred, who would have died waiting for a treatment that had not yet been proven and might not have worked."

"Might not have," she said.

"Preliminary research has a well-documented yield. Between eight and fifteen percent of promising early-stage work produces clinically viable results under ideal conditions. The conditions were not ideal." He said this plainly, as someone stating a rate. "I weighed the certainty of continued deaths against the contingency of a cure that might not materialize. The archive decision followed from that calculation."

She looked at him. She had been in rooms like this before — the careful reasoner, the organized defense, the argument that held up on its own terms. She had been good at finding where it didn't hold.

She was having difficulty finding where this one didn't hold.

---

"The research might have led somewhere," she said.

"It might have."

"You don't know it wouldn't."

"I don't." He did not look away from her. "That's what a window of uncertainty is. I worked inside it, as I have always worked, because certainty was not available. The archive decision was made under the same conditions as every other decision in this program. We do not have the luxury of waiting for certainty. Certainty arrives after the people who needed it have died."

She heard Eli in this, which was not fair to either of them. She set Eli aside.

"There's a second argument," she said. "You didn't suppress it only for the calculation."

For the first time in the conversation, something shifted in him. Not discomfort — more like the adjustment of someone who has reached the part of the argument they find most important.

"The transplant program is not a contingency measure," he said. "It is not what we do in the absence of something better. It is the correct direction — for this species, in this world, on the trajectory we are on. If you cured ash lung tomorrow, you would have extended the biological window. That is all you would have done. The environment is becoming less habitable with each decade. The biological substrate is fragile. It was fragile before the wars and the wars made it more so. The program does not solve a temporary problem. It solves the underlying one."

"You believe that."

"I have believed it for twenty-three years. The research outcome doesn't change it."

"So the archive decision was simpler than the calculation."

"No," he said. "It was more complex. The calculation was genuine. The belief is also genuine. They arrived at the same conclusion. I am not surprised you find that convenient."

---

He turned his chair toward the terminal. He didn't open anything. He was looking at something she couldn't see.

"The people who were transferred in the early years," he said. "You know what the data shows about calibration. About the communication patterns."

She knew.

"The resistance calls it loss," he said. "They call it drift. They use language that assumes the human behavioral register is the measure of what persists." He turned back toward her. "When language becomes shorter, they say: the person is diminishing. When the transplanted withdraw from common spaces, they say: something is going. I look at the same data and I see: a consciousness successfully integrating a new sensory apparatus. The pattern continues. It operates differently, through different means. The sensorium is merely the interface."

She heard it. The same line, same words. She had written it in her notebook in the briefing room. She had told herself it cost her nothing.

In the archive room, three days ago, she had sat with the data and thought about the freeze. Eli mid-sentence. Eli going still. Eli leaving through the access corridor without completing the word he was reaching for.

She did not say this. She looked at Harlan and did not say it.

---

She picked up the drive from his desk.

He watched her do it without moving.

"What will you do with it," he said. It was not the question it sounded like. He was asking because he was a precise man and wanted to know where the argument was going, not because he was afraid of the answer.

She didn't answer.

She stood.

"You've been looking," he said, "for the place where it doesn't hold."

She said nothing.

"I have been looking at the same place for twelve years," he said. "I know where it doesn't hold. I know what it costs. I knew it when I filed the note and I know it now." He straightened the materials on his desk. "I did not make this decision carelessly. I made it carefully. That is different from making it wrongly."

---

She left without telling him what she would do.

---

She stood in the corridor for a moment after the door closed. The administrative level was occupied at this hour — staff in the glass-walled offices on either side, someone carrying materials toward the elevator. Normal. The Institute's upper level going about the normal work of keeping the program running.

She had come in with the drive and the note at the bottom of the file and the certainty that finding the thing was enough. That it had a shape that didn't have another explanation.

It still had that shape. She still knew what the note meant.

She also knew — and this was the part she had not prepared for — that the window of uncertainty was real. That the cost of false hope was something she had watched, in her work, people absorb. That the man she had just spoken with had thought about this longer than she had and had not found the thing that made it simple.

She had not found it either.

She took the elevator down. The lobby. The rain.

She had the data. She had the conversation she had just had. She walked out into the rain and neither of them was enough to know what to do, and the one thing she was certain of was that she had not been certain of enough things for the last three days to start trusting certainty now.

She walked toward the bridge. Somewhere below the grating, the drainage channel was running fast and dark.

She crossed it.
