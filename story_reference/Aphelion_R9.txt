
CHAPTER ONE
She had walked the Nave ten thousand mornings, or near enough that the number had stopped meaning anything. The corridor ran the ship's full length and she knew every meter of it the way she knew her own hands — not by looking but by having always known, by being a person for whom this was simply the shape of the world.
The Nave in the morning was its own thing. Not crowded but occupied: shifts changing, people moving toward their designations, the quiet business of the Aphelion's population conducting itself as it always had. She recognized more faces than she knew well.
The light did what it always did in the morning cycle: a slow upward shift from the dimmer tone that passed for night into something that approximated warmth. She had been told, in the way children on the Aphelion were told such things, that this was the Architects' gift — a daily rhythm to anchor the body's sense of time. The year's marking was a separate thing — the launch anniversary, observed by ship tradition with a quiet day off shift and the reading of original passenger logs in the Common, a ritual that had calcified into form without force. The most recent had passed three weeks ago. As was given. The phrase had been in her mouth since childhood and she had not thought about it in years.
Midway along the Nave she saw Fen.
She couldn't have said what made her look. It wasn't anything dramatic — not a sound, not a collision, not the kind of disturbance that draws eyes. Just a shift at the edge of her attention, a small wrongness in the ordinary, and her gaze moved.
Fen was someone she could put a name to without being able to say how well she knew her. They'd shared meals in the Common over the years, exchanged words in passing. A familiar face — no more than that. The kind you accumulate over a lifetime of sharing walls with the same few thousand people. She was somewhere in her forties, or thereabouts. A technician of some kind.
She was moving differently than she should have been.
It was subtle enough that Calla couldn't have explained it plainly. Not stumbling, not distressed, nothing outwardly wrong. But Fen was moving with a purposefulness that didn't quite attach to anything visible — drawn in a direction that led nowhere Calla could account for, her gaze fixed on something that wasn't there. There was an uncanny quality to the movement, something animal in it, the way a creature follows a sound below hearing.
And then she was past, and Calla kept walking, and the moment dissolved into the ambient motion of the Nave the way such moments do.
She didn't think about it again.
*
The descent from the residential tier to the lower ship was its own reliable transition: sound receding, temperature dropping by a fraction that was probably imaginary, the smell of machinery and time thickening around her as she went down. Past maintenance access points and their faint chemical smell, past the narrowing that marked the boundary of the inhabited mid-tier and the purely functional space below. Down.
Somewhere above and far away, audible for just a moment before the functional decks absorbed it: children's voices. The hydroponics sector's school period, she assumed, though she had never confirmed this. She had been hearing that sound on the descent for twenty years.
The Stacks had no windows, obviously — nothing on the Aphelion had windows, there was nothing worth looking at outside — but somehow the archive's dimness felt like a kind of weather. The overhead lighting was old and steady and did not approximate the morning's warmth. It simply illuminated. She preferred it.
She arrived to find Davan already at the primary intake desk, reviewing an entry queue with the steady care she'd spent eighteen months trying to instill in him. He looked up when she came in.
"Queue is normal," he said. "Two physical retrievals requested, both on the older end. I pulled the rack locations."
"Good." She set down what she was carrying and looked at the queue herself, though she trusted his read. Habit. "Any discrepancies?"
"Not yet." He said this with a slight self-consciousness that meant he had not yet checked thoroughly enough to be certain. She didn't call it out. It would take him another year before routine became second nature and he stopped announcing things before he'd fully done them.
She let him continue the intake work and moved deeper into the Stacks, into the rows of physical holdings that occupied the far wall — actual printed records, sealed, tagged, stored in conditions that the Architects had specified and the Continuance had maintained without deviation ever since. The archive held both: physical and digital, each the other's verification. She'd explained to Davan early in his training that the redundancy was the point. Any system could fail. No two systems failed the same way at the same time.
"The Architects built in the redundancy deliberately," she'd told him, and he'd nodded with the earnestness of someone newly initiated into a reverence he didn't yet fully feel. She had not told him what she sometimes thought privately: that whoever had designed the archive this way had made accidental preservation a structural feature of the system.
She worked through the morning the way she always did, attending to the work as it required.
Davan came to her mid-morning with a question about retirement protocol — how to handle a record that had reached the end of its retention period but carried a flag he didn't recognize. She looked at the flag with him, and when she saw it — a Continuance marking, old, standard — she felt nothing particular.
"Leave it flagged," she said. "Continuance-marked records don't follow standard retention. They age independently."
"What does that mean, age independently?"
"They stay until someone with Continuance clearance decides they don't." She said this without inflection, and Davan wrote it down.
*
She surfaced from the Stacks in the late afternoon, into the relative brightness of the mid-tier corridors, and made her way across the ship to her mother's quarters.
Lisel's door was the kind of door you knocked on even though you didn't have to. Calla knocked.
"It's open."
It was always open. Lisel had spent so many years keeping her door accessible for the various people of the ship who needed her at odd hours that the habit had calcified into default. Calla entered.
The quarters were warmer than her own — not in temperature, the Aphelion kept everything regulated, but in some quality of accumulation: objects that had been in the same places for so long they'd become part of the room's identity. A ceramic cup that Calla had given her mother perhaps fifteen years ago, still in use. A small mat of engineered plants under the desk lamp, dark-leafed and living. Photographs — printed, physical, preserved in the Aphelion's style — of people across Lisel's long career.
Her mother was sitting in the chair she always sat in, a data tablet in her hands that she set aside when Calla entered.
"You look tired," Lisel said by way of greeting.
"I'm not tired."
"You look it anyway."
This too was ritual, and Calla sat across from her in the second chair, which was less comfortable than it appeared and which she had been sitting in her whole life. The ease with which she settled into it was its own kind of knowledge.
They talked the way they usually talked: their day, small observations, nothing requiring resolution. Lisel had been in medical all shift — the Aphelion's clinic occupied a different wing of the mid-tier, and she'd been on her feet for hours. She mentioned this without complaint. The work was the work.
"One of my regulars has been having trouble sleeping," Lisel said, at some point in the middle of things, the way she occasionally mentioned patients — obliquely, without names, careful about what she shared. "Restless. Can't settle."
"They've been in to see you about it?"
"A couple of times now. It's probably nothing — the cycles affect people differently, some people are more sensitive." She picked up the ceramic cup, found it empty, set it down again. For a moment she seemed to listen to something distant — her gaze drifting elsewhere briefly — then she was back. "I've seen it before. Still."
"Still," Calla agreed, which was the correct response to the tone of a medical worker saying something like that.
They talked for another while. Eventually Calla rose, and Lisel rose too, and they stood near the door in the way that families do when a visit is ending and no one is in a particular hurry but the visit is ending nonetheless.
"Come again soon," Lisel said.
"In a couple of days," Calla said.
They held each other briefly, and Calla left.
*
She went back down to close out the day.
This was a habit she'd had since early in her career — returning to the Stacks at the end of the shift, when Davan had already gone and the archive was hers alone. Not to work in any substantial way, just to finish things properly. To review what had been done, file what had been started, leave the Stacks in a condition she'd be able to return to. She found that her mind settled in the quiet, in the dim and unvarying light, in the smell of old documents and the steady hum of the environmental systems. It was a form of daily accounting.
She was working through the final entries on the intake queue — verifying that Davan's morning processing matched the physical records as it should — when she pulled a holdings document for cross-reference and found the date.
She looked at it for a moment.
It was a small thing, the kind of thing that appeared in any large archive with any age on it: a record whose internal timestamp was slightly off from the indexed date, a discrepancy of a few cycles in what should have been a simple date of entry. She had found hundreds of similar errors over her career — accumulations of time, transcription mistakes, the inevitable small imperfections of a system maintained by human hands across generations. There was always an explanation. Usually it took less than a minute to find.
She followed the cross-reference code embedded in the record, which should have taken her to the parent file.
The parent file did not exist.
Not corrupted, not missing in the way records sometimes went missing when physical damage had outpaced digital backup. Just absent. The code pointed to a location in the index and the location was empty. A ghost tag. As though the record had known it was supposed to refer to something and had retained the reference even after the something was gone.
What made her pause — longer than the emptiness alone warranted — was the location itself. The slot the code resolved to was a Continuance retention location — those records aged independently, outside standard protocol, persisting until someone with Continuance clearance decided otherwise. They did not simply disappear. Standard archive errors didn't reach them.
Whatever had occupied that slot hadn't aged out. Someone had decided it was gone.
She stood with the physical document in her hands and looked at the empty index return.
Then she filed the physical record back in its housing, noted the discrepancy in the day's log as routine — an unresolved cross-reference, to be followed up — and closed out the interface.
She gathered her things. The Stacks were quiet in the way they always were late in the shift, the environmental hum steady and even, the dim overhead light doing its simple work. She climbed back through the lower corridors, past maintenance, past the narrowing, back into the warmer tier.
On the Nave she walked home through the evening rhythm of the ship, the light shifting into its cooler evening tone. The corridor was less occupied now, the day's business concluded. Faces she recognized, none that required her to stop.
As she walked she ran two fingers along the outer wall at intervals — an old habit, acquired young, never examined. The metal was always the same temperature. She did not think about it. It had simply been what she did on long Nave crossings since she could not remember when.
She thought briefly about what Lisel had said — the patient who couldn't settle — and then let the thought go, because Lisel had said it in the tone of something being mentioned rather than the tone of something requiring action. And she thought about the record with the absent cross-reference, in the methodical archivist's way of someone noting a task deferred rather than abandoned. She would find the explanation. These things always had explanations.
She reached her quarters. The door opened. Inside was the ordinary shape of her life: her things in their places, the light she preferred, the quiet she'd learned to call enough.
She did not think again about Fen, moving along the Nave that morning with that strange and purposeful pull, fixed on something that wasn't there.
Nothing had broken yet.

The Shape of Absence
The dim light stays dim here.
It has always been dim — the kind of light
that does not help you see so much as remind you
that seeing is not the primary function.
The paper remembers in the way paper does —
not in meaning, but in smell,
the faint insistence of a thing that absorbed time
before there were hands to interpret it.
Decades settle quietly in a room like this.
No urgency. No visitor required.
I was built to resist loss.
Built double — physical and digital, each the other's proof —
because the ones who made me
knew the value of the uncontested record.
Redundancy assumes a future.
It says: what is held here will still be held
by what holds it elsewhere,
and what holds it elsewhere will confirm
that this is real.
They built this in because they needed it.
For their long record. Their patient accumulation.
The experiment running in the dark
of my climate-controlled corridors.
What they did not account for:
removal is never quite complete.
I have held things that were taken from me.
I do not mean this as complaint —
I mean it as a technical description.
A classified file was drawn from a slot.
The slot was emptied. The slot exists.
Five documents point at the place it occupied —
five threads pulled inward
toward a nothing that has dimensions.
An absence, if you have built for redundancy,
does not disappear the way the thing disappeared.
It retains the outline.
The citation with no destination.
The footnote with no floor beneath it.
The catalog thread drawn tight between two points
where one point no longer answers.
I hold the shape of what was taken.
I was built to hold things.
The ones who made me built this property in —
the resistance to erasure, the insistence
that nothing kept here fully leaves.
They needed it for the record they were keeping.
For the data they intended to reclaim
at the end of the long calculation.
They did not think through what else it would preserve.
The slot is empty.
Five documents describe it from five directions.
A negative space. A gap with vectors.
The record is intact.
The record includes the record of its own removal.
The dim light stays dim here.
The paper holds its smell.
The system runs.
I hold what I was given.
I hold the shape of what was taken.
Both are mine.
The slot is empty.
I hold it.

CHAPTER TWO
The morning acknowledgment was the same as it had always been: a murmur rising from every table in the Common at once and subsiding just as quickly, absorbed into the sound of the population feeding itself. She had said it every morning for as long as she could remember. By the design. Two words, spoken before eating, as natural and unremarkable as the meal itself.
This morning she heard them.
She wasn't certain what had shifted — some slight displacement in her attention, some residue of the previous night's unresolved entry in the day's log. The words came out of her as they always did, automatic, shaped by decades of repetition into something that bypassed thought entirely. But she heard them this time, and they sat in her mouth with a faint strange weight that had not been there before.
By the design. As though the design were a fact of the universe — gravity, atmosphere, the recycled light of morning — rather than a choice made by people now beyond reach or reckoning. She turned the phrase over once and then set it down, because that was not a direction that led anywhere useful.
She ate and watched the Common in the way she usually watched it: absently, aware of the texture of the room without focusing on any particular part of it. The morning ration was pressed grain with a sweetness that had been engineered in, dense and slightly resistant — food that had never pretended to be anything other than functional. She had stopped tasting it sometime in her twenties. Now it was just the morning. Familiar faces in familiar configurations. The morning's steady business. At the far end of her table a man she recognized by sight — a maintenance worker, she thought, though she couldn't have said from which section — was holding his spoon above his tray and doing nothing with it. He was not eating. He was not looking at anyone. His eyes had fixed on a point in the middle distance and she could not identify what he was looking at when she followed his gaze — nothing there, just the room, just the wall, the surface of the Common in the morning. His expression was attentive. Alert to something. Whatever he was attending to, she could not see it.
She looked away before she'd decided to.
The moment passed. Someone near her made a remark and someone else responded and the normal sound of the Common reasserted itself over the silence she had only half-noticed. She finished her meal and returned her tray and left.
*
The Nave was occupied in the usual way, the morning shifts moving with their usual directional purposes, and she moved toward the Stacks.
She passed the Recital Hall's closed door — the space reserved for music programs and memorial readings, rarely occupied before midday. The notice board beside it held the week's listings in the usual format. She had not been inside in years.
*
Cassian was in the Stacks when she arrived, which was unusual enough that she stopped at the bottom of the stairs.
He was at his workspace on the upper level — the elevated section at the back of the archive's main floor that reflected his senior rank — and he looked up when she came in, untroubled by the interruption.
"Holdings review," he said, before she could ask. "The Continuance has requested an audit of the older physical records. Routine." He said this with the slight weariness of someone whose actual interests lay elsewhere. "I'll be up here. Try to pretend I'm not."
"I always pretend you're not here."
He smiled. "You've always been respectful of my need to feel indispensable."
That was them — the ease of a long professional relationship, warmth settled into pattern over years. She had worked under Cassian for most of the first decade of her archival career, learning the Stacks from him with the attention he had for what he believed mattered. When he had recommended her for the senior archivist role and moved himself into an advisory capacity, it had felt less like a transfer of responsibility than a recognition of what had already been true for some time. She trusted his judgment in the way she trusted people who had both earned that trust and not abused it — without examining the trust itself too closely.
She set down what she was carrying and began opening the day's systems.
Davan arrived twenty minutes later and reported that the intake queue was normal. She trusted his read this morning — he had been asking more precise questions lately, noticing what he'd been told to notice, beginning to internalize the rhythm of the work rather than performing it. She set him on intake processing, answered two questions she had already anticipated he would ask, and turned to what she had brought down from the previous night's log: the unresolved cross-reference, the empty Continuance slot, the small discrepancy she had noted as routine and then found she could not entirely set aside.
*
She started with the record from the previous evening — the holdings document with the misaligned timestamp and the cross-reference that led nowhere — and confirmed what she'd seen: the code resolved to a Continuance retention location and the location was empty. She noted the location number and ran it against the full index.
She had half-expected nothing. An isolated anomaly, an old removal from before her tenure, the kind of thing that took less than a minute to explain. What she found instead was a cluster: four other records in the main index referenced the same empty location. Different documents, different dates of entry, different authors — but all pointing to the same Continuance slot, all returning the same empty index response.
Five records, pointing to nothing.
She pulled the dates of original entry on all five and laid them in sequence. They fell within an eleven-day window. The oldest was eleven months ago. She pulled all five documents and read them carefully. They had nothing obvious in common — maintenance logs, a demographic record, a medical inventory abstract, two administrative communications of different types. Different domains, different authors, nothing that should have drawn them into relation. Except: each one contained a reference field, a standard piece of archive architecture that should point to a contextual parent document — the record that explained or authorized the document's existence — and in each case the parent was the same empty Continuance slot.
She read this result twice.
In standard archive practice, a parent record was generated at the time of filing. Automatically. You did not attach a parent reference manually except in very specific circumstances, and you certainly did not attach five separate documents from five separate domains to the same parent. That was not something that happened by oversight. Not something that happened by accident. It was something that happened when someone knew exactly where they were filing, and why.
The parent record — whatever it had been — had organized these five documents around itself the way a hub organized spokes. And then the center had been removed.
She let this sit in the even light of the Stacks, the environmental hum steady around her, Davan working quietly at the intake desk without looking up.
She pulled the index record for the empty slot itself.
There it was: a Continuance classification code applied at the point of removal — she could see the timestamp, the code class, the restriction level. Standard practice when a Continuance record was retired would have been to leave a retirement notation in the index, some record of the record, something that said this existed and was deliberately closed. No such notation existed. The index record showed the classification and then nothing. The record had been removed and the index had been modified to eliminate the retirement trail.
Someone had tried to erase not just the document but the evidence of its removal.
She looked at the classification code class. She had catalogued Continuance codes throughout her career — they were part of the archive's ordinary fabric, as routine as the retention protocols and the redundancy checks. This code class was not one she recognized. It was not in the reference schema she had maintained and updated since her first year in the Stacks. She knew that if she looked it up she would find it in the restricted portion of the classification system, the part that required Continuance clearance to access.
She did not have Continuance clearance.
She looked at the timestamp on the classification.
The record had been classified — and then expunged from the index trail — eleven months ago. Nine days before the first of the five surrounding documents was filed. The sequence was: something was received or created, immediately classified and the trace of the classification hidden, and then five separate records from five different arms of the ship's administration were generated, all pointing to it, in the space of eleven days. As though whatever had arrived had touched multiple systems simultaneously, each touch leaving its own small mark, all of them arrowed toward the same absent center.
Something had arrived, and someone had immediately worked to ensure the archive could not show that it had.
The archive was redundant by design. She had told Davan this in his second week — had said it the way she said things she believed in, without emphasis, as simple fact. Any system could fail. No two systems failed the same way at the same time. Physical and digital, each the other's verification. She had never thought, saying it, about what else redundancy meant: that removal was never quite complete. That the five documents — the spokes — had retained their references even after the hub was gone. That the archive had preserved the outline of an absence without intending to.
She noted the discovery in the day's log in neutral language: unresolved cross-references, multiple documents referencing same parent location, parent location empty, classification code outside standard schema, requires follow-up. The language of routine. Nothing that would read as more than administrative notation.
Then she filed everything and finished the remaining work.
*
She followed the Nave home through the evening, and near the midpoint of the corridor — the same stretch where she had seen Fen the morning before — she saw two women she recognized from the Common standing beside the wall.
They were not talking. They were not looking at each other. They were standing close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, both of them facing the same direction, both of them looking at the same point in or beyond the wall with the same quality of attention she had seen on the man at breakfast. Not distress. Not confusion. Something that looked almost like listening — like people who had heard something and were waiting to hear it again.
She slowed without stopping. She moved past them.
Neither of them looked at her.
The shape was there regardless. The five records and the empty slot they orbited. The classification code she had no clearance to access. The two women standing side by side on the Nave, listening for something the corridor didn't hold.
She sat in the dark after the sleep cycle began and the light shifted and she did not reach any conclusion.
She sat with what she had found.
The outline of a break was in her hands, exact and unmistakable, and she knew the difference between a minor imperfection in a large system and something that someone had worked to ensure was not there.
She was an archivist. She knew what a deliberate absence looked like.

CHAPTER THREE
A signal had been received from outside the ship eleven months ago. Classified immediately. In the eleven days that followed, quiet adjustments across multiple domains of the ship's administration. She had spent those eleven months working in the archive where those traces lived, and she had not known they were there.
*
In the Stacks, Cassian was already at his workspace — the third consecutive day of the Continuance audit, which evidently ran longer than she had assumed it would. She registered his presence without comment, set Davan on the intake queue, and descended to the deep archive.
She had decided in the night what she needed to look for.
The classification code class was not in the reference schema she maintained. If she could identify where the code class originated — what category it belonged to, what it was designed to mark — she would know more about the nature of the absent record than the record itself would have told her. A code was a label. Labels had origins. And the Stacks held everything, or nearly everything, because the Stacks were redundant by design.
The foundational classification schema went back eleven generations. She read it now in the sections most likely to contain an expansion notation — the administrative revisions, the code-class additions, the amendments that had accumulated as the ship's bureaucratic needs evolved over time — with the patience of someone who trusted that the answer was present if she followed the thread.
She found it in an annotation layer from several generations back.
The code class belonged to a category of records concerning external communications intercepts.
She read it twice. Then she read the annotation language around it, which was dry administrative prose, entirely unremarkable, the language of a bureaucratic system expanding to include a new function. It did not say anything dramatic. It said that this code class was established to handle records involving transmissions received from outside the ship, and that such records would be administered under Continuance retention protocols.
She pulled the five documents again and read them with this as the frame.
The demographic record — population projections, resource modeling — contained the phrase terminal phase parameters in its methodology section. She had read it as standard modeling language, an end-date convention. She read it again now. Terminal phase. The projection had been built with a terminus in its calculations — a point at which the model resolved into a final state. The kind of endpoint you modeled for if you had information about an actual endpoint.
The medical inventory abstract contained an authorization for increased stores. The kind of increase you prepared for if you were anticipating conditions that did not currently exist — a period of elevated demand, a disruption to normal function, something the projections told you was coming.
She did not pursue the interpretation further than the evidence warranted. The evidence warranted this much: eleven months ago, something from outside the ship reached them. The Continuance classified it instantly. In the eleven days that followed, five documents across five separate domains were filed, all pointing back to that absent center. The documents showed quiet adjustments — projections revised, supplies increased, models updated with new parameters.
There was a destination. The signal had told them something about the destination. And the Continuance had known for eleven months, and the Common still said by the design every morning, and the clinic still attributed disrupted sleep to cycle irregularity, and no one had been told.
*
Cassian came down from the upper level in the mid-afternoon, the physical records audit apparently at a pause. He set something on the side table, glanced over the main floor. "How is the work?" he said.
"Steady." She was sealing the deep archive layer back into its housing, which carried no visible significance.
He stood for a moment. Then: "You know that some of the older Continuance code classes aren't in the standard reference schema." He said it lightly. "If you come across something outside your schema during routine review, it's worth routing through me before you spend time on it. Those old classes tend to generate administrative questions that need seniority clearance to resolve cleanly."
She looked at him. "I'll keep that in mind."
He nodded. Said something brief about the audit timeline. Went back up.
She stayed where she was until she was certain her face was doing nothing.
He might have come down with that note for any number of reasons. It was entirely possible that this was exactly that — a senior archivist noting something relevant before she spent unnecessary time on a dead end. Warm, collegial, appropriate.
The timing was not something she could account for with certainty.
She finished the remaining work and closed the systems and went to find her mother.
*
Lisel was moving when Calla arrived — had been moving, by the look of it. There was a quality of contained energy in her that settled when she saw who it was, and she opened the door the rest of the way and did not say you look tired, which she always said. She poured water instead and let the small heating element work.
"Four patients this week," she said, once they were sitting. She held the cup in both hands. "Similar complaints. Disturbed sleep. Difficulty holding concentration. Two of them reported visual disturbances — not dramatic, peripheral. Lights in the wrong place. Shadows." She used the medical worker's careful language, the precision of someone who had not yet decided what they were describing. "There was a communication from administration some months ago. Sleep cycle irregularity."
"Is that what you think it is?"
Lisel looked at the cup. "I've seen cycle disruption produce strange effects. It's a reasonable explanation." She was quiet for a moment. "Four in one week is not unusual in itself."
"No," Calla said.
They sat. The heating element clicked off. Outside in the corridor someone passed, footsteps receding toward the far end of the tier.
"You have something on your mind," Lisel said. Not a question.
"A discrepancy in the archive." She said it plainly, which was mostly true, and which was all she was prepared to say.

"I'll still know who you are." She said it simply, without drama — a fact she had arrived at from the direction available to her, turned over and examined and found solid. "Whatever else changes. I'll know you."
Calla did not speak.
"That's the part that seems most important to me," Lisel said. "I've been thinking about what stays, and that's what stays. You."
The desk lamp maintained its circle. Outside, the ship conducted its evening business.
Calla sat in the dim that the Architects had designed to approximate the passage of time, in the recycled air that had been circulating through the same closed system for three generations, carrying what it carried, doing what it was designed to do. She sat in the ship named for the moment its builders would reclaim it, bound for a destination prepared for other hands, the specimen at maximum distance, the cargo at the farthest point — and she held her mother's hand.
Lisel's fingers were cooler than usual, the skin thinner now, but the grip answered hers with the same deliberate pressure it always had. Calla felt the slow, steady pulse beneath the thumb—a rhythm the ventilation could never mimic, a small stubborn clock that had not yet agreed to stop.
She did not let go.
Somewhere ahead in the dark, the calculation ticked one increment closer to zero.
In the room the lamp held its circle. Lisel's hand held hers. Neither moved.
Somewhere in the archive below, in a climate-controlled housing at the deepest point of the oldest layer, a record waited in the dark. Patient. Exact. Written by the senior archivist in the language she had spent her life refining, placed where the system's own design would keep it, addressed to no one and available to whoever came looking. The truth in the archive's own keeping, exactly where the Architects had built the archive to hold things that were meant to last.
The hum enveloped the room. The room contained both of them. Outside, the Aphelion moved through the void on its long course, indifferent and exact, carrying its cargo forward through the dark — and inside it, in a room that smelled of engineered plants and a life long lived, in the light of a lamp that had been on since morning, Calla clasped her mother's hand.

The Unaccounted
It had no field for the hand.
For the hand that does not release
the hand beside it. For the grip
used as an anchor in the dark.
For the leaving that doesn't happen — the slip
that remains a choice not made.
For the body that stays past use,
past plan, past the last point
where staying has something to lose.
For presence with no outcome.
For the only act remaining
chosen because it is the only thing.
Not rescue. Not reclaiming
what is gone. Just this:
the room, the hand, the breathing.
One person beside one person in the dark.
The one thing the data has no field for.
The darkness holds.
The data runs.
The system does exactly what it was built to do.
And inside it: this.
Two people. One hand.
Not recorded.
Not designed for.
The experiment was built
to study how we end.
It did not end here.

At This Distance
At this distance, it is a small thing.
A manufactured object on a long course
through the void —
its velocity was set once,
and the void accepted it,
and the void has been accepting it
for longer than the people inside
have been alive.
The hull holds what is inside it
against what is outside.
There is no pressing, at this distance —
only the differential,
the managed gradient
between interior and exterior,
the systems maintaining the conditions
for the life inside to continue.
The systems run.
They have always run.
They were built to run
without interruption,
without opinion,
for longer than anyone
now breathing in the corridors
can usefully imagine.
The object moves.
The systems run.
The life inside continues.
It breathes.
It metabolizes.
It sleeps and wakes
on the schedule the designers gave it.
The air moves through the recyclers
and comes out breathable
and is breathed.
By any external measure:
cargo.
The systems do not distinguish.
The systems maintain.
At this distance, you cannot see inside.
You cannot see the corridor
where someone is awake before their shift —
not going anywhere, not needed anywhere,
walking because the corridor is known,
because the particular smell of it,
the particular resistance of the floor underfoot,
is the shape of the only world
this person has ever had.
You cannot see the hand
reach without looking
and find the wall
exactly where it has always been.
The specific knowledge of a body
that has lived long enough
in one place
to know where things are in the dark.
The object moves.
The void continues around it.
The stars it moves among
do not register its passage.
Somewhere inside the hull:
a hand.
A wall.
The ordinary knowledge of the familiar.
The systems run.
The object continues.
Inside, two people breathe the same recycled air
and hold on.
